# Heuristics — Andrzej Marczewski (HEXAD User Types)

## Decision Framework

Before selecting any gamification mechanics, profile your user base against the six HEXAD types: Philanthropist, Socialiser, Free Spirit, Achiever, Player, and Disruptor. Design mechanics that serve your top 3 user types. Any mechanic that does not map to a specific user type motivation is guesswork.

---

## H1: Know Your Types Before You Design
- **Rule:** Never select mechanics without understanding your user type distribution. A leaderboard for a Philanthropist-dominated audience is wasted effort. A gifting system for a Player-dominated audience will not move the needle. Profile first, design second.
- **When to apply:** At the start of every gamification design initiative, before any brainstorming of features or mechanics.
- **Example:** A healthcare app discovers its user base is 40% Philanthropist, 25% Socialiser, 20% Achiever, 15% other. Design primary mechanics around helping others (Philanthropist) and community (Socialiser), not leaderboards (Achiever/Player).
- **[SOURCE: Andrzej Marczewski, Even Ninja Monkeys Like to Play, Ch. 3]**

## H2: The HEXAD Is Not Bartle
- **Rule:** HEXAD user types are gamification-specific, designed for real-world product motivation. Unlike Bartle's types (designed for MUDs/virtual worlds), HEXAD types map to how people respond to gamification in non-game contexts. Do not confuse Achievers in games with Achievers in gamification — the contexts are fundamentally different.
- **When to apply:** When team members reference Bartle's taxonomy to justify gamification design decisions.
- **Example:** A Bartle "Killer" in an MMO disrupts other players for fun. A HEXAD "Disruptor" in a gamified system pushes boundaries to find system improvements. Same name, entirely different motivational dynamic and design response.
- **[SOURCE: Andrzej Marczewski, Even Ninja Monkeys Like to Play, Ch. 2]**

## H3: Players Are Not Bad — They Are Your On-Ramp
- **Rule:** The Player type (extrinsically motivated, responsive to rewards) is not inferior to intrinsic types. Players are the most responsive to gamification mechanics and often the quickest adopters. Design initial engagement for Players, then create pathways that transition them toward intrinsic motivation types.
- **When to apply:** When designing initial engagement mechanics and user acquisition gamification.
- **Example:** A corporate learning platform launches with points and rewards (attracting Players). Over time, it introduces mentorship features (Philanthropist pathway) and creative challenges (Free Spirit pathway). Players who transition become deeply engaged; those who remain Players still contribute value.
- **[SOURCE: Andrzej Marczewski, Even Ninja Monkeys Like to Play, Ch. 5]**

## H4: Disruptors Are Your Quality Assurance
- **Rule:** Disruptors push boundaries, find exploits, and challenge the rules — and that is valuable information. They reveal weaknesses in your gamification system before those weaknesses become widespread problems. Rather than fighting Disruptors, channel their energy into innovation contests, beta testing, and system improvement feedback.
- **When to apply:** When users are "gaming the system" or finding loopholes in your gamification mechanics.
- **Example:** A sales gamification platform has Disruptors who find ways to earn points without actual sales activity. Instead of adding more rules, create a "system improvement" challenge where identifying and reporting exploits earns recognition and influence over the rules.
- **[SOURCE: Andrzej Marczewski, Even Ninja Monkeys Like to Play, Disruptor chapter]**

## H5: Multi-Type Design Is Non-Negotiable
- **Rule:** Any system that only serves 1-2 HEXAD types alienates 50-70% of potential users. Design mechanics that serve at least your top 3 user types. The minimum viable mechanic mix should address Achievers (progress), Socialisers (connection), and one other type relevant to your audience.
- **When to apply:** During mechanic selection and portfolio design for any gamification system.
- **Example:** A fitness app with only achievements and leaderboards serves Achievers and Players but ignores Socialisers (need community), Philanthropists (need to help others), and Free Spirits (need exploration). Add team challenges, mentoring features, and customizable workout paths.
- **[SOURCE: Andrzej Marczewski, Even Ninja Monkeys Like to Play, Ch. 7]**

## H6: Behavioral Signals Over Self-Report
- **Rule:** Do not ask users what type they are — observe their behavior. Philanthropists help without being prompted. Achievers complete all available challenges. Free Spirits explore every feature. Socialisers create connections. Players check reward availability first. Behavioral classification is far more accurate than survey-based self-report.
- **When to apply:** When building user type profiling into your analytics and personalization systems.
- **Example:** Track early user behaviors: users who immediately explore settings and customization are likely Free Spirits. Users who immediately check the leaderboard are likely Players or Achievers. Users who immediately message or connect with others are Socialisers. Use these signals, not survey answers.
- **[SOURCE: Andrzej Marczewski, HEXAD survey research and behavioral profiling methodology]**

## H7: Type Migration Is Real and Desirable
- **Rule:** Users can and do shift HEXAD types over time. A Player who starts for extrinsic rewards may become an Achiever motivated by mastery, or a Socialiser motivated by community. Design systems that support and encourage this migration by offering deeper engagement mechanics alongside extrinsic reward mechanics.
- **When to apply:** When designing long-term engagement mechanics and lifecycle management.
- **Example:** A community platform starts with badges for participation (Player mechanic). After users accumulate social connections, it introduces mentoring roles (Philanthropist mechanic) and community leadership (Socialiser mechanic). Users who migrate to intrinsic types have higher lifetime engagement.
- **[SOURCE: Andrzej Marczewski, Even Ninja Monkeys Like to Play, type migration concept]**

## H8: Intrinsic Motivation Is the Gold Standard
- **Rule:** Extrinsic mechanics (points, badges, rewards) are the bridge. Intrinsic motivation (autonomy, mastery, purpose, relatedness) is the destination. A gamification system that never transitions users from extrinsic to intrinsic motivation is sustainable only as long as you can afford to fund rewards. Intrinsic engagement is self-sustaining.
- **When to apply:** When evaluating the long-term sustainability of a gamification system's design.
- **Example:** Wikipedia has almost zero extrinsic rewards. Contributors are motivated by purpose (Philanthropist), mastery (Achiever), and community (Socialiser). This intrinsic-first design is why Wikipedia has sustained engagement for over 20 years with minimal gamification mechanics.
- **[SOURCE: Andrzej Marczewski, Even Ninja Monkeys Like to Play, intrinsic motivation chapters]**

## H9: The Mechanics-User Type Mapping
- **Rule:** Each HEXAD type responds to specific mechanic families. Philanthropists respond to gifting, mentoring, and knowledge-sharing. Socialisers respond to teams, chat, and collaboration. Free Spirits respond to customization, exploration, and creative tools. Achievers respond to challenges, certifications, and mastery levels. Players respond to points, leaderboards, and prizes. Disruptors respond to innovation challenges and system influence. Use the mapping; do not guess.
- **When to apply:** When selecting specific mechanics for each user type in your design.
- **Example:** For a Philanthropist audience, implement a mentoring system and a knowledge-sharing forum. For a Free Spirit audience, implement customizable dashboards and exploratory learning paths. Never swap these — each type needs its own mechanic family.
- **[SOURCE: Andrzej Marczewski, HEXAD mechanics mapping framework]**

## H10: Ethical Gamification Designs for the User's Benefit
- **Rule:** Gamification is ethical when it helps users achieve their own goals. It is exploitative when it manipulates users against their interests for business extraction. The test: would removing the gamification make the user worse off? If yes, it is ethical. If the user would be better off without it, it is manipulation.
- **When to apply:** At every ethical review checkpoint and when evaluating proposed mechanics.
- **Example:** A savings app gamification that helps users save more money = ethical (removing it makes users worse off). A mobile game gamification that drives compulsive spending through artificial urgency = exploitative (removing it makes users better off).
- **[SOURCE: Andrzej Marczewski, Ethical gamification principles, blog and talks]**

## Anti-Patterns

| Anti-Pattern | Why It Fails | HEXAD Diagnosis |
|---|---|---|
| One-type-fits-all mechanics | Serves 15-25% of users; alienates the rest | Multi-type design ignored (H5) |
| Leaderboards for Philanthropist audiences | Philanthropists are motivated by helping, not competing | Type-mechanic mismatch (H9) |
| Fighting Disruptors with more rules | Creates escalation; wastes Disruptor insight | Disruptors misunderstood (H4) |
| Self-report type surveys as sole input | Users self-report aspirational types, not actual types | Behavioral signals needed (H6) |
| Only extrinsic mechanics with no intrinsic pathway | Sustainable only with ongoing reward funding | No type migration support (H7) |
| Treating Player type as the entire audience | Ignores 4 intrinsic types; creates shallow engagement | Player overweighting |
| Applying Bartle types to gamification | Wrong framework for non-game contexts | HEXAD ≠ Bartle (H2) |
