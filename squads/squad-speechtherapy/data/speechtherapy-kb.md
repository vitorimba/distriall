**Scope:** Vocal rehabilitation, nodule recovery, singer care, voice hygiene
**Primary reference:** Evidence-based speech-language pathology literature

## 1. Vocal Anatomy Basics

### 1.1 The Larynx

The larynx (voice box) sits in the anterior neck at the level of C3-C6 vertebrae. Its primary
biological function is airway protection, but its secondary function — phonation — is the one
that matters for singers and voice professionals.

Key cartilages:
- **Thyroid cartilage** — the largest, forms the front wall ("Adam's apple")
- **Cricoid cartilage** — ring-shaped, sits below thyroid, foundation of laryngeal skeleton
- **Arytenoid cartilages** — paired, pyramid-shaped, control vocal fold abduction/adduction
- **Epiglottis** — leaf-shaped, deflects food during swallowing

Key muscles:
- **Thyroarytenoid (TA)** — body of the vocal fold, controls stiffness and thickness
- **Cricothyroid (CT)** — stretches the vocal folds, raises pitch
- **Posterior cricoarytenoid (PCA)** — only abductor, opens glottis for breathing
- **Lateral cricoarytenoid (LCA)** — adducts folds for phonation
- **Interarytenoid (IA)** — closes posterior glottis

### 1.2 The Vocal Folds

The vocal folds are layered mucoviscous structures approximately 17-23mm long (male) and
12-17mm long (female). Structure layers from superficial to deep:

1. **Epithelium** — squamous epithelial covering, protective outer shell
2. **Superficial lamina propria (SLP)** — loose fibrous layer, allows cover vibration (Reinke's space)
3. **Intermediate lamina propria (ILP)** — primarily elastin fibers
4. **Deep lamina propria (DLP)** — primarily collagen fibers
5. **Vocalis muscle (TA)** — the body

The cover-body model (Hirano, 1974) divides this into:
- **Cover** = epithelium + SLP (vibrates freely)
- **Transition** = ILP + DLP
- **Body** = vocalis muscle

For phonation, the cover vibrates over the body in a mucosal wave. Nodules form in the
**junction of the anterior and middle thirds** of the fold — the point of maximum mucosal wave
displacement and therefore maximum trauma from collision.

### 1.3 Resonators

Sound produced at the larynx (source) is shaped by the vocal tract (filter):
- **Pharynx** — primary resonating cavity, most controllable
- **Oral cavity** — shaped by tongue, jaw, lips
- **Nasal cavity** — adds nasality and nasal resonance
- **Chest** — lower register resonance (not a primary acoustic resonator but felt as vibration)
- **Sinus cavities** — minor acoustic contribution

Formants (F1, F2, F3...) are resonant frequencies of the vocal tract. Skilled singers learn
to tune formants to enhance specific harmonics.

### 1.4 The Phonatory Mechanism

Voice is produced by:
1. **Respiratory drive** — subglottic air pressure builds below closed glottis
2. **Bernoulli effect + tissue elasticity** — folds are blown open then snap closed
3. **Mucosal wave** — travels from inferior to superior surface of fold
4. **Acoustic result** — periodic pressure wave = sound

**Phonation Threshold Pressure (PTP):** The minimum subglottic air pressure required to
initiate and sustain phonation. Critical concept for nodule pathophysiology — nodules raise
PTP, requiring more effort to phonate.

## 2. Vocal Nodules

### 2.1 Definition

Vocal nodules (also called "singer's nodules" or "screamer's nodules") are bilateral,
symmetrical benign lesions of the vocal fold cover (SLP). They form at the junction of the
anterior and middle thirds of both vocal folds — the site of maximum collision force.

Histology: early nodules are edematous (fluid-filled), soft; mature nodules are fibrotic
(collagen-dense), firm.

### 2.2 Stages of Development

**Stage 1 — Acute / Soft Nodules**
- Duration: weeks to months
- Consistency: edematous, soft, translucent
- Symptom severity: mild to moderate
- Reversibility: HIGH — respond well to voice rest and therapy
- Appearance: small, sessile swellings, often appear after vocal overuse

**Stage 2 — Subacute / Transitional**
- Duration: months
- Consistency: mix of edema and early fibrosis
- Symptom severity: moderate
- Reversibility: MODERATE — therapy effective, may take longer (12+ weeks)
- Appearance: more defined, slightly opaque

**Stage 3 — Chronic / Hard Nodules**
- Duration: years of unaddressed abuse/misuse
- Consistency: fibrotic, firm, opaque/white
- Symptom severity: moderate to severe
- Reversibility: LOW — surgery often required before therapy
- Appearance: keratotic, clearly demarcated bilateral masses

### 2.3 Causes and Pathophysiology

Primary mechanism: **repeated microtrauma from excessive vocal fold collision forces**

Contributing factors:
- Vocal hyperfunction (excessive muscular tension during phonation)
- Dehydration (raises PTP, reduces mucosal wave efficiency)
- Acid reflux / LPR (laryngopharyngeal reflux) — inflammatory change to epithelium
- Allergies / post-nasal drip — mucus on folds increases friction
- Shouting, screaming, loud sustained singing
- Incorrect vocal technique (pressed phonation, pushing from chest)
- Smoking / environmental irritants

### 2.4 Risk Factors for Singers

- Singing in incorrect tessitura (too high or too low for voice type)
- Inadequate warm-up before performance
- No cool-down after extended singing
- Singing through illness (upper respiratory infection, laryngitis)
- Dehydration (touring, air conditioning, low humidity environments)
- Excessive performance schedule without adequate vocal rest
- Using chest voice in upper register transitions
- Emotional singing style with excessive laryngeal tension
- Insufficient technical training — poor support, improper resonance placement

### 2.5 Symptoms

- Hoarseness / rough voice quality
- Breathiness (air escape through incomplete glottal closure)
- Pitch breaks, especially at passaggio
- Fatigue with prolonged speaking or singing
- Reduced upper register range
- Effortful phonation ("voice has to be pushed")
- Loss of dynamic control (difficulty singing piano/pianissimo)
- Throat clearing and globus sensation (feeling of something in throat)
- Increased PTP (voice does not "kick in" easily)

## 3. SOVT — Semi-Occluded Vocal Tract Exercises

### 3.1 Science Foundation (Titze, 2006)

Semi-Occluded Vocal Tract (SOVT) exercises place the vocal tract in a configuration where the
mouth opening is partially or fully occluded. This creates an acoustic back-pressure that
interacts with the glottis.

**Key acoustic mechanism:**
When the vocal tract is semi-occluded, the inertive reactance of the supraglottic system
increases. This raises the ratio of glottal flow to subglottic pressure, allowing the vocal
folds to vibrate with LESS collision force while maintaining the same acoustic output.

In plain terms: SOVT lets you make voice with less effort and less fold impact.

### 3.2 Why SOVT Is Therapeutic for Nodules

- Reduces phonation threshold pressure (PTP)
- Decreases collision force between fold masses — reduces microtrauma
- Promotes symmetric mucosal wave
- Improves fold medial compression without hyperfunction
- Provides proprioceptive feedback (vibration felt in lips, mask)
- Trains easy onset without pressed phonation

### 3.3 SOVT Exercise Types

**Lip trills (bilabial trills):**
Blow air through loosely closed lips while phonating. One of the most effective SOVT exercises.
Start with glides up and down the scale.

**Tongue trills:**
Roll the "R" sound (lingua-alveolar trill) while phonating. Useful if patient cannot lip trill.

**Straw phonation:**
Phonate through a thin cocktail straw (4-5mm diameter). Titze's most studied SOVT protocol.
Progressions: straw in air → straw in water → straw while reading aloud.

**Lip buzz / raspberry:**
Similar to lip trill but with more air pressure. Good for warming up support.

**Humming with lip closure:**
Hum with lips closed, tongue relaxed. Forward resonance, minimal laryngeal effort.

**Nasal consonants with vowels:**
"Mmmm-ah", "nnnn-ay" — transitions from nasal semi-occlusion into open vowel.

**Cup phonation:**
Phonate into a paper cup held loosely over mouth/nose. Creates variable occlusion.

### 3.4 Straw Phonation Protocol (Titze Standard)

Session structure:
- 5 minutes straw in air (glides, arpeggios, scales)
- 5 minutes straw in water (bubbling — visual feedback)
- 5 minutes straw phonation while reading text
- Progress to 15-20 minutes total over 4 weeks

Frequency: 2-3x daily during rehabilitation phase

## 4. VFE — Vocal Function Exercises (Stemple, 1994)

### 4.1 Methodology

VFE is a systematic program of gentle, precise exercises designed to strengthen and balance the
laryngeal musculature and improve the efficiency of the phonatory mechanism. Developed by
Joseph Stemple at the University of Kentucky.

Philosophy: treat the voice like any other muscle group — systematic, progressive loading with
adequate rest.

### 4.2 The 4 Core Exercises

Performed 2x daily (morning and evening), each set done twice:

**Exercise 1 — Warm Up:**
Sustain the vowel /i/ (as in "see") as softly as possible on the musical note F4 (women) or
F3 (men) for as long as possible. Goal: maximize sustained phonation time.
Purpose: establishes easy, low-effort phonation baseline.

**Exercise 2 — Stretch:**
Glide from the lowest note possible to the highest note possible on the word "knoll" (or
"whoop" for children). Smooth, continuous glide, no breaks.
Purpose: stretches CT muscle, improves upper register access.

**Exercise 3 — Contract:**
Glide from the highest note possible to the lowest note possible on the word "boom".
Smooth, continuous descent.
Purpose: contracts TA muscle, builds lower register strength.

**Exercise 4 — Adductory Power:**
Sustain the musical notes C, D, E, F, G (do-re-mi-fa-sol) on "ol" (as in "old") — one note
per breath, each for as long as possible.
Purpose: builds sustained adductory power, improves glottal closure efficiency.

### 4.3 Evidence Base

Multiple RCTs demonstrate VFE efficacy:
- Stemple et al. (1994): significant improvement in phonation time, intensity, and jitter/shimmer
- Roy et al. (2003): VFE > vocal hygiene for teacher voice disorders
- Pasa et al. (2007): VFE effective for nodules and polyps pre- and post-surgery

### 4.4 Progression and Monitoring

Weeks 1-2: Learn correct form, establish baseline measures
Weeks 3-6: Increase sustain duration per exercise
Weeks 7-12: Add dynamic variations (crescendo/decrescendo on sustained notes)
Monthly: Re-measure MPT, pitch range, voice handicap index (VHI-10)

## 5. LMRVT — Lee Silverman Voice Treatment Resonant Voice (Katherine Verdolini Abbott)

### 5.1 Methodology

Also known as Lessac-Madsen Resonant Voice Therapy (LMRVT) or simply Resonant Voice Therapy
(RVT). Developed by Katherine Verdolini Abbott, building on Lessac's and Madsen's resonant
voice work.

Core concept: **resonant voice** — a voice quality characterized by anterior oral vibrations
(felt in the "mask": lips, nose, cheekbones) produced with minimal laryngeal effort.

Resonant voice is hypothesized to be produced just at or slightly above the threshold of
complete vocal fold adduction — the "easy vibratory" zone that maximizes acoustic output
relative to collision force.

### 5.2 Theoretical Basis

The "continuum of adductory behavior" (Verdolini Abbott):
- Breathy voice: under-adduction, incomplete closure, high airflow, low collision
- Resonant voice: optimal adduction, complete closure with minimal compression force
- Pressed/strained voice: over-adduction, high compression, high collision force, tissue trauma

Nodules push the voice toward pressed phonation to compensate for incomplete closure.
LMRVT trains the voice back toward the resonant zone.

### 5.3 Core Exercise — "Nasal Glide"

1. Start with a "mmm" hum — feel vibration in lips and face
2. Add "mmmm-one, mmmm-two..." counting on breath
3. Progress to "mmmm-ay, mmmm-oh, mmmm-ee" (nasal + vowel pairs)
4. Progress to reading sentences starting with nasal sounds: "Many men make money..."
5. Progress to spontaneous speech maintaining the forward "buzz" sensation

Tactile cue: put fingers on lips — you should feel gentle vibration throughout.

### 5.4 Session Structure (LMRVT)

Each session (30-45 min):
- 5 min: "Easy" vocal warm-up — sighs, glides
- 10 min: Nasal glide exercises (humming progressions)
- 10 min: Carryover into structured sentences/reading
- 10 min: Conversational carry-over with coaching
- 5 min: Home practice instructions

Practice frequency: 5 days/week during intensive phase (weeks 1-6)

## 6. CTT — Conversation Training Therapy (Gartner-Schmidt, 2014)

### 6.1 Methodology

Developed by Jackie Gartner-Schmidt at the University of Pittsburgh Voice Center.
CTT focuses on transferring learned vocal behavior directly into conversational speech —
the most ecologically valid context for voice use.

Traditional voice therapy often excels in structured exercises but fails in carry-over to
real daily communication. CTT addresses this gap by using natural conversation as the
primary treatment medium from the first session.

### 6.2 Core Principles

1. **Minimal instruction** — few biomechanical cues, focus on sensory awareness
2. **Immediate conversational context** — therapy occurs IN conversation, not before it
3. **Continuous feedback** — therapist provides real-time acoustic and perceptual cues
4. **Self-monitoring training** — patient learns to detect and self-correct in real time
5. **Generalization first** — designed so learned behavior transfers immediately

### 6.3 Key Techniques

**The "Easy Onset":** Initiate voice gently, without hard glottal attacks. Vowel-initial
words especially — "I" should be soft, not punched.

**Conversational resonant voice:** Apply the resonant voice target (from LMRVT) directly in
open conversation, not just exercises.

**Rate and pacing:** Controlled speech rate reduces vocal loading. Pause more, rush less.

**Breath management in conversation:** Renew breath frequently. Never speak on residual air.

### 6.4 Use Case for Singers

CTT is most valuable when:
- Singer has learned healthy technique in singing but reverts to hyperfunction in speech
- Singing voice has recovered but speaking voice remains abusive
- Patient is a teacher, presenter, or high-demand speaker
- Carry-over from singing therapy to daily voice use is poor

## 7. Manual Laryngeal Therapy (MTD — Lieberman)

### 7.1 What Is MTD

Musculoskeletal Tension Disorder (MTD) describes voice disorders caused primarily by
excessive tension in the perilaryngeal and paraglottic musculature rather than by structural
vocal fold pathology. MTD often co-exists with nodules or develops in response to them
(compensatory tension).

### 7.2 Manual Laryngeal Massage (Lieberman Protocol)

Developed by Jacob Lieberman (Charing Cross Hospital, London). Involves:
- Digital palpation of laryngeal musculature
- Circumlaryngeal massage to reduce hypertonic tension
- Laryngeal repositioning (lowering hyoid-laryngeal complex)
- Myofascial release of thyrohyoid membrane and strap muscles

Signs of laryngeal elevation and tension:
- High laryngeal position (thyroid notch near hyoid)
- Tenderness on palpation of thyrohyoid space
- Restricted laryngeal mobility laterally
- Decreased distance between hyoid and thyroid cartilage on palpation

### 7.3 Therapeutic Effects

- Immediate reduction in vocal effort after successful laryngeal repositioning
- Decreased PTP following tension release
- Improved mucosal wave symmetry
- Psychological benefit: patient feels the change instantly (unlike exercise-based therapies)

### 7.4 MTD Assessment Criteria

Lieberman's 5-point palpatory assessment:
1. Tenderness of the body of the hyoid
2. Tenderness of the thyrohyoid space (superior thyroid border vs hyoid)
3. Tenderness of the posterior thyroid border
4. Laryngeal elevation (reduced thyrohyoid space)
5. Restricted lateral laryngeal movement

Score 3+ out of 5 = significant MTD component, manual therapy indicated.

## 8. Vocal Hygiene Fundamentals

### 8.1 Hydration

**Surface hydration:** The vocal folds must be covered by a thin mucus layer for efficient
vibration. Dehydration raises PTP and increases fold stiffness.

Recommendations:
- 8+ glasses of water daily (2-2.5 liters)
- Increase intake in dry environments (air conditioning, heated rooms, aircraft)
- Warm herbal teas (non-caffeinated) are beneficial — warmth loosens mucus
- Avoid: caffeine (diuretic), alcohol (diuretic + local irritant), carbonated drinks

**Systemic hydration vs. surface hydration:**
Drinking water does not directly wet the vocal folds (water goes to stomach). It hydrates
systemically, supporting mucosal secretion. Direct humidification (steam inhalation, personal
humidifier) provides surface hydration more directly.

Steam inhalation: 10-15 minutes with portable steam inhaler before demanding vocal tasks.
Saline nasal spray: helps maintain mucosal health throughout the day.

### 8.2 Nasal Care and Sinus Health

The nose humidifies, filters, and warms incoming air before it reaches the larynx. Bypassing
nasal breathing (mouth breathing) delivers dryer, cooler, unfiltered air to the vocal folds.

Nasal irrigation (neti pot / sinus rinse):
- Clears excess mucus and allergens
- Reduces post-nasal drip (a major source of vocal fold irritation)
- Isotonic saline solution: 240ml warm water + 1/4 tsp non-iodized salt + pinch baking soda
- Frequency: 1-2x daily during active sinus issues; 3-4x/week maintenance

Post-nasal drip management:
- Identify and treat allergies (allergist referral if chronic)
- Avoid throat clearing — use swallowing or "silent cough" instead
- Nasal steroid sprays (Flonase etc.) if allergic component confirmed by physician

### 8.3 Voice Rest

**Complete voice rest:** No phonation whatsoever. Reserved for acute vocal hemorrhage,
acute severe laryngitis, or post-surgical recovery. NOT routinely prescribed.

**Relative voice rest (conservation):**
- Reduce overall talking time by 50-75%
- No whispering (whisper = high subglottic pressure, abductor position — harmful)
- No shouting, no loud singing
- Avoid competing with noise (bars, parties, sporting events)
- Strategic silence: complete rest periods interspersed with necessary speech

For singers with nodules: relative voice rest + targeted therapy more effective than absolute
rest, which leads to muscle deconditioning.

### 8.4 Environmental Factors

Harmful:
- Dry air (below 40% relative humidity) — raise room humidity with humidifier
- Smoke (active and passive) — primary irritant and carcinogen
- Chemical fumes, dust, cleaning products
- Excessive air conditioning or heating without humidification
- Loud environments requiring voice elevation (teaching in noisy rooms, working in restaurants)

Protective:
- Room humidifier (target 50-60% relative humidity)
- Personal steam inhaler for touring singers
- Saline nasal spray in dry or air-conditioned environments
- Minimize throat-clearing habit

### 8.5 Medications Affecting Voice

Drying effects (increases PTP, reduces mucosal secretion):
- Antihistamines (especially first-generation: Benadryl)
- Decongestants (pseudoephedrine, phenylephrine)
- Antidepressants (tricyclics, SSRIs to lesser degree)
- Diuretics
- Anticholinergics

Helpful:
- Mucolytics: guaifenesin (Mucinex) — thins mucus, reduces viscosity
- Steroid nasal sprays (for allergic rhinitis)

Always consult prescribing physician before modifying medications. Voice concerns should be
communicated to all treating physicians.

## 9. Singer-Specific Considerations

### 9.1 Vocal Warm-Up for Singers

Purpose of warm-up:
- Increase blood flow to laryngeal musculature
- Gradually increase mucosal pliability
- Establish muscle memory for optimal phonatory posture
- Psychological preparation

Recommended warm-up sequence (20-30 min before performance):
1. 5 min: Body warm-up — gentle stretch, posture alignment, breath work
2. 5 min: SOVT exercises (lip trills, straw phonation) — lowest effort start
3. 5 min: Humming in comfortable middle range
4. 5 min: Scales on mixed vowels (not full voice yet)
5. 5-10 min: Repertoire in lower-demand sections, building to full voice

Signs of inadequate warm-up: pitch breaks, restricted upper range, effortful onset, excessive
throat clearing need.

### 9.2 Cool-Down After Performance

Often neglected. Critical for recovery, especially after extended performance.

Cool-down sequence (10-15 min post-performance):
1. Gentle descending glides ("whoop" downward)
2. Lip trills or straw phonation in medium volume
3. Humming in lower register
4. Finish with easy sighs and silence

Purpose: gradually release muscular tension, promote circulation/recovery in laryngeal tissues,
reduce residual edema.

### 9.3 Performance Demands and Vocal Loading

Concept of "vocal dose" (Titze): cumulative vibration exposure of vocal folds over time.
Like physical dose of exercise — too much without recovery = injury.

Managing performance schedule:
- Track total voice-on time per day
- Heavy performance days require light speech days before and after
- Touring: prioritize hydration, humidification in hotels, sleep
- Avoid speaking over background noise after performances
- Learn to identify fatigue signals: pitch instability, increased effort, hoarseness

### 9.4 Return to Singing After Nodules

A graduated return is essential — jumping back to full performance after therapy risks relapse.

General progression (assuming nodule resolution on stroboscopy):
- Week 1-2: 15-20 min gentle singing, no upper register, piano dynamic only
- Week 3-4: 30 min, begin easy mid-range transitions, no performances
- Week 5-6: 45 min, include full range but no sustained high notes
- Week 7-8: 60 min, begin rehearsals (no full voice performances yet)
- Week 9-12: Graduated return to full performance with monitoring

Clearance criteria:
- Stroboscopy: bilateral mucosal wave restored, no visible nodular mass
- MPT: within normal limits for sex/age
- VHI-10: below threshold (<18)
- Patient reports no fatigue, effortless phonation

## 10. 12-Week Rehabilitation Timeline Overview

This timeline reflects the protocol for Stage 1-2 vocal nodules in singers. Stage 3
(fibrotic) nodules may require prior surgical intervention.

### Phase 1 — Foundation (Weeks 1-3)
**Goals:** Establish vocal hygiene, introduce SOVT, reduce hyperfunction
**Key activities:**
- ENT/stroboscopy evaluation (medical clearance)
- Vocal hygiene education (hydration, nasal care, environment)
- Relative voice rest protocol established
- SOVT exercises introduced (lip trills, straw phonation 2x daily)
- Laryngeal manual assessment; manual therapy if MTD present
- Baseline measures: MPT, CPP, VHI-10, pitch range

### Phase 2 — Exercise Introduction (Weeks 4-6)
**Goals:** Begin VFE, start resonant voice work
**Key activities:**
- VFE program introduced (2x daily, all 4 exercises)
- LMRVT nasal glide exercises
- SOVT continued, progressions added
- CTT component: easy onset in structured speech
- Re-evaluation at week 6: stroboscopy or acoustic measures

### Phase 3 — Consolidation (Weeks 7-9)
**Goals:** Strengthen gains, begin vocal loading
**Key activities:**
- VFE progressions (increasing sustain times)
- Full LMRVT carryover into spontaneous speech
- CTT: conversational monitoring in daily contexts
- Gentle return to singing: 15-30 min sessions per day
- Singer-specific technique work: passaggio, resonance placement

### Phase 4 — Return to Full Function (Weeks 10-12)
**Goals:** Full return to vocal performance, prevention plan
**Key activities:**
- Graduated singing return (see Section 9.4)
- Performance warm-up/cool-down protocol established
- Long-term hygiene plan finalized
- Final stroboscopy and acoustic outcome measures
- Discharge criteria met: VHI-10 < 18, normal stroboscopy, patient-reported confidence

## 11. When to Seek Medical Intervention vs. Therapy

### Medical Intervention First (ENT referral required)

- Suspected vocal fold hemorrhage: sudden onset severe hoarseness after vocal effort, "pop" sensation
- Suspected laryngeal cancer: unexplained hoarseness > 3 weeks, smoking history, dysphagia, otalgia
- Recurrent respiratory papillomatosis (RRP): progressive hoarseness in young adults or children
- Suspected paralysis/paresis: breathy voice, aspiration, glottic gap on scope
- Stage 3 fibrotic nodules: surgery prior to therapy generally required
- Acute laryngitis with complete voice loss: medical management (not voice therapy)
- Subglottic stenosis, laryngeal masses, cysts: all require ENT assessment first

### Therapy Appropriate as Primary Intervention

- Stage 1-2 nodules with clear vocal abuse/misuse history
- Muscle tension dysphonia (MTD) without structural lesion
- Functional dysphonia (no visible lesion)
- Puberphonia / mutational falsetto
- Mild polyps (some) — many resolve with therapy
- Post-surgical voice rehabilitation (after ENT clearance)
- Prevention programs for professional voice users

### Red Flags for Urgent Referral

- Sudden onset complete aphonia with normal prior voice: psychogenic vs. neurological
- Odynophonia (painful phonation): possible inflammatory, neoplastic, or infectious process
- Stridor or respiratory distress: immediate ER referral
- Dysphagia + voice change: rule out pharyngeal/laryngeal malignancy
- Voice change + neurological symptoms (tremor, dysarthria): movement disorder workup

## 12. Glossary of Key Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Adduction** | Closing of the vocal folds toward midline for phonation |
| **Abduction** | Opening of the vocal folds for breathing |
| **Aphonia** | Complete absence of voice |
| **Dysphonia** | Any disorder of voice (hoarseness, breathiness, strain, etc.) |
| **Formant** | Resonant frequency peak produced by the vocal tract filter |
| **Fundamental Frequency (F0)** | Rate of vocal fold vibration; perceived as pitch |
| **Glottis** | Space between the vocal folds |
| **Hard glottal attack** | Abrupt, forceful initiation of voicing on vowel-initial words; traumatic |
| **Hoarseness** | Rough, irregular voice quality due to aperiodic vocal fold vibration |
| **Jitter** | Cycle-to-cycle variation in period (pitch perturbation); elevated in pathology |
| **Laryngoscopy** | Visualization of the larynx using rigid or flexible endoscope |
| **LPR** | Laryngopharyngeal reflux — acid/pepsin reaching larynx; causes inflammation |
| **Maximum Phonation Time (MPT)** | Longest duration /a/ can be sustained on single breath; measure of glottal efficiency |
| **MTD** | Musculoskeletal Tension Disorder — hypertonicity of laryngeal/neck muscles |
| **Mucosal wave** | Undulatory surface motion of vocal fold cover during phonation; visible on stroboscopy |
| **Passaggio** | Transitional register zone between chest and head voice in singing |
| **Phonation Threshold Pressure (PTP)** | Minimum subglottic pressure needed to initiate phonation |
| **Pressed phonation** | Tightly adducted, high-effort voice; damaging to fold tissue |
| **Shimmer** | Cycle-to-cycle variation in amplitude; elevated in pathology |
| **SOVT** | Semi-Occluded Vocal Tract — exercises with partial mouth occlusion to reduce fold collision |
| **Stroboscopy** | Visualization of vocal fold vibration using strobe light; gold standard for nodule assessment |
| **Supraglottic activity** | Constriction or hyperfunction of structures above the glottis (false folds, arytenoids) |
| **Tessitura** | The range most comfortable and natural for a given voice type |
| **VFE** | Vocal Function Exercises — systematic laryngeal strengthening program (Stemple) |
| **VHI-10** | Voice Handicap Index (10-item) — validated self-report of voice impact on quality of life |
| **Vocal loading** | Cumulative physiological stress placed on the vocal mechanism over time |
| **Voiced onset** | How voice initiation occurs: breathy, simultaneous, or hard attack |
